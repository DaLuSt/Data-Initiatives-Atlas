# Euro 7

> **Added 2026-08-28, `verification: primary-source` from creation.** Five
> sources were read directly this pass — three on `eur-lex.europa.eu`, the
> European Parliament's own legislative file, and an industry association's
> status page — before this entity was written, rather than compiled from
> search snippets and verified later. This is the Atlas's first entity for
> Euro 7 (Regulation (EU) 2024/1257), which is adopted and in force as a
> legal instrument but **has not yet come into effect** in the operative
> sense: none of its type-approval requirements bind manufacturers until
> 29 November 2026.

## Description

Regulation (EU) 2024/1257 of the European Parliament and of the Council of
24 April 2024 governs "type-approval of motor vehicles and engines and of
systems, components and separate technical units intended for such
vehicles, with respect to their emissions and battery durability" —
confirmed by reading its own text on `eur-lex.europa.eu` directly. It is
commonly known as **Euro 7**, though the regulation's own text uses that
name only parenthetically in its title; it does not rename the emissions
class structure itself.

It was adopted following a Commission proposal of **10 November 2022**
(confirmed via the European Parliament's own legislative file, procedure
reference **2022/0365(COD)**), published in the Official Journal on
**8 May 2024**, and **entered into force on 28 May 2024** — the twentieth
day following publication, per its own Article 21.

### What it consolidates and adds

Confirmed by reading the European Parliament's own legislative summary
directly: Euro 7 **merges the previously separate emission rules for cars
and vans (Euro 6) and lorries and buses (Euro VI) into one consolidated
framework** — the first time these two regimes have shared a single legal
instrument. Confirmed by reading `eur-lex.europa.eu`'s own text directly,
**Article 20** repeals six prior instruments in full: Regulation (EC) No
715/2007, Regulation (EC) No 595/2009, Regulation (EU) 2017/1151,
Regulation (EU) No 582/2011, Regulation (EU) 2017/2400, and Commission
Implementing Regulation (EU) 2022/1362.

Beyond consolidation, it extends EU vehicle-emissions law into territory it
did not previously cover:

- **Non-exhaust emissions.** For the first time, EU law limits particle
  emissions from **brakes and tyres**, not only exhaust pipes. Confirmed by
  reading the regulation's own recital 20 directly: "Emissions from tyres
  are estimated to be the largest source of microplastics released into the
  environment... those non-exhaust emissions should therefore be measured
  and limited." Article 14(4) requires implementing acts to establish
  measurement methods for both.
- **Battery durability.** Article 4(6)(d) requires vehicles to carry
  **state-of-health (SOH) monitors** for the traction battery. Article 3
  defines SOH as "the measured or estimated state of a specific performance
  metric of a vehicle or traction battery at a specific point in its
  lifetime, expressed as a percentage of the performance that was
  determined when certified or new" — confirmed by reading the regulation's
  own text directly. Minimum performance thresholds are set out in Annex II.
- **Extended durability testing.** Confirmed by reading the European
  Parliament's own legislative summary: in-service conformity verification
  is extended to **200,000 km and 10 years**, doubling the distance and
  duration required under the Euro 6/VI regime it replaces.

## The data provisions — why this belongs in a data-initiatives atlas

Confirmed by reading `eur-lex.europa.eu`'s own text of the regulation
directly, three linked mechanisms give Euro 7 a genuine data-governance
dimension, not just an environmental-standards one:

1. **On-board fuel and energy consumption monitoring (OBFCM).** Article
   4(6)(c) requires manufacturers to equip vehicles with "OBFCM devices to
   monitor their real-world fuel and electric energy consumption and other
   relevant parameters." Article 6(7) requires these devices to be "capable
   of communicating all legally required relevant vehicle data they record,
   via the OBD port and over the air." Regulation (EU) 2024/1257 extends
   OBFCM's scope to pure electric vehicles for the first time — previously
   it covered only combustion and hybrid vehicles running on liquid fuels.
2. **On-board monitoring (OBM) and real-driving compliance.** Article
   6(6)(b) requires OBM systems capable of "communicating data on exhaust
   emission performance and battery durability data of the vehicle via the
   OBD port, including for the purpose of roadside inspections, and
   anonymously over the air for the purpose of monitoring compliance of
   vehicle types." This is a live, continuous compliance-monitoring data
   channel, not a one-off type-approval test.
3. **The Environmental Vehicle Passport (EVP).** Article 7(4) requires
   manufacturers to "issue an EVP for each vehicle and deliver that
   passport to the purchaser together with the vehicle, extracting the
   relevant data from sources, such as the certificate of conformity and
   the type-approval documentation." Article 3's definition 68 confirms the
   EVP must disclose "pollutant emission limits, CO2 emissions, fuel
   consumption, electric energy consumption, electric range and engine or
   electric motor power, and battery durability" — a standardised,
   consumer-facing environmental-performance data artifact for every new
   vehicle, comparable in concept to the digital product passports emerging
   elsewhere in EU product law.

Article 4(11) additionally requires manufacturers to "ensure the secure
transmission of data related to emissions and battery durability by taking
cybersecurity measures in accordance with UN Regulation No 155" — the
UNECE vehicle-cybersecurity type-approval regulation. **No relationship to
[[UN-UNECE]] is asserted**: UN Regulation No 155 is a distinct technical
instrument under the 1958 UNECE Agreement, not itself modelled as an Atlas
entity, and the cross-reference is recorded in prose only.

Confirmed by reading `eur-lex.europa.eu`'s own text directly, **no article
names the European Commission, the JRC, the EEA, or any other EU body as
the designated recipient of aggregated OBFCM or battery data** — the
regulation requires the data to exist, to be communicated off-board and
over the air, and to reach the vehicle's own passport, but does not itself
establish a central EU database along the lines of the [[EU-DGA]] or
[[EU-EMDS]] data-space model. Whether such a central reporting mechanism is
established by a separate implementing act was not researched this pass.

## ⚠ Not yet in effect — and why `status: adopted` rather than `active`

Euro 7 has been a binding legal instrument since 28 May 2024, but none of
its substantive type-approval obligations apply yet. Confirmed by reading
`eur-lex.europa.eu`'s own text directly, application is staggered by
vehicle category:

| Category | New types | All new vehicles |
|---|---|---|
| M1/N1 (cars, vans) | **29 November 2026** | 29 November 2027 |
| M2/M3/N2/N3/O3/O4 (buses, trucks, trailers) | 29 May 2028 | 29 May 2029 |
| Small-volume manufacturers, M1/N1 | 1 July 2030 | — |
| Small-volume manufacturers, M2/M3/N2/N3 | 1 July 2031 | — |

As of this entity's creation (2026-08-28), the earliest application date —
29 November 2026 for new M1/N1 type-approvals — has **not yet arrived**.
This is `status: adopted` under this Atlas's own controlled vocabulary
(`metadata/controlled-vocabularies.md`): "a formally adopted instrument
that is not yet in force... or an act adopted with deferred application,"
sitting between `proposed` and `active`. `start_date` records the legal
entry-into-force date (28 May 2024), not the later date its requirements
begin to bind.

### The original proposal's timeline moved

Confirmed by reading the European Parliament's own legislative file
directly: the Commission's original 2022 proposal targeted **1 July 2025**
for light-duty vehicles and **1 July 2027** for heavy-duty vehicles — both
roughly 16-22 months earlier than the dates the enacted regulation actually
sets. The legislative history (proposal 10 November 2022; provisional
agreement 18 December 2023, confirmed independently by AECC's own status
page; final adoption 24 April 2024) pushed the effective dates back rather
than forward, the opposite direction to the Digital Omnibus-style
postponements recorded elsewhere in this Atlas for the AI Act
([[EU-AI-ACT]]) — worth noting as a genuinely different pattern: here, the
delay was baked into the original enactment, not a later amendment.
